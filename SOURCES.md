# Sources and leads — Thessaloniki

Starting points, not a finished reference list. Verify everything before it
goes on a poster, and cite in the style the course expects.

## Open data

| Source | What is there |
| --- | --- |
| [opendata.thessaloniki.gr](https://opendata.thessaloniki.gr/en) | Municipal open data portal (DKAN). Patchy and partly stale, but it has geospatial road files, municipal police complaint records, and budget execution. Interface is bilingual; most dataset descriptions are Greek only. |
| [data.gov.gr — Δήμος Θεσσαλονίκης](https://data.gov.gr/organization/dimosthessalonikis) | The same municipality's datasets on the national portal, including `.shp`/`.dxf` street base layers |
| [statistics.gr](https://www.statistics.gr) | ELSTAT. The 2021 census is the authoritative population source and publishes down to settlement level as spreadsheets |
| [github.com/OpenThessaloniki](https://github.com/OpenThessaloniki) | Civic-tech group. Repos are archived, but the crime-metrics and points-of-interest projects show what data existed and who was working with it — potentially a stakeholder contact |
| OpenStreetMap | Best base geometry for the city. Overpass Turbo will export venues, vacancies and amenities as GeoJSON if you want a layer built from data rather than by hand |

## Figures to put in the table

From the 2021 census: the **municipality** of Thessaloniki has 319,045
residents across 19.3 km², a density of roughly 16,500 per km². The
**metropolitan area** recorded 1,006,112; the wider **regional unit**
1,092,919.

Which of those three you use matters, and saying which one you mean is itself a
mark of careful work — most student reports quietly mix them. The municipality
is about a third of the built-up urban area, so "Thessaloniki has 320,000
people" is misleading on its own.

## The 1997 legacy — the academic core

This is the strongest part of your case, because the failure is documented
rather than merely alleged.

- **Deffner, A. & Labrianidis, L. (2005)**, "Planning culture and time in a
  mega-event: Thessaloniki as the European City of Culture in 1997",
  *International Planning Studies* 10(3–4), 241–264. The central text. Argues
  the programme lacked strategic and time planning, spent heavily on flagship
  buildings, and had no city-marketing or tourism policy to carry it forward.
- **Labrianidis, L. (2011)**, "Thessaloniki's arrested development: missed
  opportunities", *Antipode*. Widens it from the programme to the city's
  political economy.
- **Palmer, R. & Richards, G. (2009)**, *European Cultural Capital Report 2*,
  ATLAS, Arnhem.

That last one is worth noting: **Greg Richards, who commissioned your project,
has published on exactly this**. Read his ECoC work before the conference and
reference it in the presentation. It is a legitimate way to show you understand
what he is actually asking for, and it will land better than a generic
literature review.

The framing that emerges: 1997 produced buildings (Concert Hall, port museums)
but not the governance or programming to sustain them, while the institutions
that thrived — the Film Festival, the photography scene — mostly predate the
programme or grew alongside it. Whether the audience it built is itself a
legacy is genuinely contested, which is exactly the sort of unresolved question
the publication wants.

Check your library access through BUas for the *International Planning Studies*
paper before you leave. If you cannot get it, ask Jörn Fricke.

## Other programmes worth mapping

- **European Youth Capital 2014** — much smaller, much less documented, and the
  gap between its promises and its traces is a good short case.
- **Nea Paralia waterfront** (Nikiforidis–Cuomo, phased from the mid-2000s) —
  the placemaking project residents actually cite. Award-winning, well used,
  and a deliberate contrast with 1997's flagship logic.
- **The metro** — opened 30 November 2024 after nearly two decades; the
  Kalamaria extension with five stations opened 27 August 2026. The
  archaeological finds displayed inside Venizelou station are a cultural-legacy
  question in their own right: infrastructure that became a museum because
  digging it revealed the city underneath.

## Where to look for stakeholders

The Municipality's culture directorate, the Film Festival organisation, the
Museum of Photography and Cinema Museum (both port-based, both 1997-adjacent),
the Aristotle University planning and geography departments, and whoever
currently runs programming on Pier 1. The university is the most likely to say
yes to students quickly.

## Practical

Nearly all municipal documents, plans and consultations are Greek only. Budget
time for translation, and put "we used machine translation for Greek sources,
checked with X" in your methods section rather than hiding it — the course
allows AI use as long as it is referenced.
